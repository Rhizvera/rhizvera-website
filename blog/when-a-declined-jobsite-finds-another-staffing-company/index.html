When a Declined Jobsite Finds Another Staffing Company

Why unsafe host employers move from one staffing provider to the next, and how to recognize one before you sign

Rhizvera · November 28, 2026

Who this is for: staffing company owners and sales leaders, and the programs that insure them.

A staffing company declines a customer. Maybe the jobsite had poorly maintained equipment, a string of injuries, or a supervisor who ignored safety rules. Maybe the staffing company’s insurer would not approve the work. The customer does not stop needing workers. It calls the next staffing company on its list.

The second staffing company sees a customer with steady volume and a workable bill rate. It does not see the injuries that happened while the first staffing company’s workers were on the floor, because those claims sit on someone else’s loss runs.

Why Each Staffing Company Sees So Little

A staffing company’s loss history tells it about its own workers. It says nothing about what happened to another provider’s workers at the same jobsite last year. A host employer with a poor record can therefore look new and clean to every staffing company it approaches.

The host employer’s own workers’ compensation history is not a full answer either. Temporary workers are usually covered under the staffing company’s workers’ compensation policy, so their injuries do not show up in the host’s own loss history. Texas law, for example, treats the staffing company’s policy as proof of coverage for both the staffing company and its client.

Warning Signs

  • The customer has used several staffing companies in a short period.
  • It is vague about why it left its last provider.
  • It wants workers on site immediately, with no time for a site visit.
  • It pushes for a bill rate that leaves little room for workers’ compensation cost.
  • It asks the staffing company to accept its class code without review.
  • It resists contract terms on safety, training or job changes.

No single sign proves a problem. Several together mean it is time for harder questions.

Questions to Ask Before You Sign

  • Which staffing companies have you used at this location in the last three years, and why did each relationship end?
  • Have temporary workers been injured at this jobsite? What happened, and what changed afterward?
  • Who supervises temporary workers day to day, and what site-specific training do they give for this equipment?
  • May we visit the jobsite, see the actual work and meet the supervisor before the first placement?

Check the Public Record

OSHA publishes inspection results by establishment. A search on the customer’s name and the jobsite’s address can show inspections, citations and the standards involved. A clean result does not prove a safe workplace, and a citation does not end the conversation, but either one is worth knowing before the first worker arrives.

Visit the Jobsite

There is no substitute for seeing the work. A short walk through the area where temporary workers will be placed shows equipment condition, housekeeping, traffic between people and vehicles, and whether the job matches what was described in the sales meeting. OSHA and NIOSH recommend that the staffing company and the host assess the worksites and tasks together before the staffing company accepts a new client.

Put It in the Contract

A client agreement should say who may change a temporary worker’s duties, require prompt notice of injuries and near misses, give the staffing company the right to inspect, and allow it to remove workers from unsafe conditions. OSHA and NIOSH also recommend that the contract state which employer is responsible for each safety and health duty. A customer that will not agree to those terms has already told you something.

Why Programs Should Care

For a carrier or program, a host employer that has rotated through several staffing companies is a concentrated risk that can enter the book through any of them. Programs that ask staffing companies to document these questions during customer review give themselves a far better chance of seeing the pattern.
