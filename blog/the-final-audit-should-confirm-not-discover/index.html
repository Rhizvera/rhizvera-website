The Final Audit Should Confirm, Not Discover

Why premium audits keep surprising staffing companies and their programs, and how monthly discipline changes the result

Rhizvera · November 28, 2026

Who this is for: staffing company owners, controllers and payroll teams, and the programs and auditors that review them.

For many temporary staffing companies, the final premium audit is the most stressful event of the policy year. The auditor arrives after the policy expires, asks for records nobody has looked at since they were filed, and produces an adjustment that can wipe out a quarter’s margin.

It does not have to work that way. A final audit should confirm what both sides already know. When it discovers something new, the reporting during the year has failed.

Why Staffing Audits Go Wrong

Staffing payroll is harder to audit than most. It is spread across many customers, jobsites, jobs and states. It changes weekly. And the class code that applies depends on the job, not on the staffing company’s own office operations.

The usual sources of large adjustments are familiar:

  • Payroll reported in a lower-rated class code than the job supported.
  • Jobs that changed during the year without a new classification review.
  • Payroll in a state that was not reported, or not listed on the policy.
  • Overtime handled inconsistently. Under NCCI’s rules, used in many states, the extra pay for overtime is excluded from the premium basis only when payroll records show overtime pay separately. When they do not, it can be counted.
  • Payroll that does not tie to the company’s quarterly federal employment tax returns (Form 941).
  • Payments to subcontractors who cannot show their own workers’ compensation coverage, which auditors can add to the premium basis, and people paid as independent contractors whose work looks like employment.

Report the Way You Will Be Audited

An auditor will want to trace payroll to the jobs that produced it. Monthly reports that show only totals by class code cannot be traced. Reports that show payroll by host employer, jobsite, job and class code can.

Reporting at that level also catches problems early. A job reporting payroll under a code it was never approved for is visible in the month it happens, not the year after.

Pick a Cut-Off and Keep It

Staffing companies pay weekly or biweekly, and pay periods rarely end on the last day of the month. Choose one rule for which pay periods belong to which month, write it down, and use it every month. A consistent cut-off makes the monthly reports add up to the year, and makes the year tie to the audit.

Reconcile Every Quarter

Each quarter, compare the payroll reported to the program with the wages on the Form 941 for every FEIN that pays workers. Differences usually have explanations: office staff, timing, bonuses, a second entity. The point is to know the explanation before the auditor asks.

Do the same each year for Forms 1099-NEC. Payments to individuals who did work like employees deserve a hard look before the audit, not during it.

A Shorter Audit

An audit that confirms is also an audit that finishes. When records are organized by job and reconciled by quarter, the auditor’s work becomes checking rather than rebuilding. Programs and staffing companies both benefit when the final adjustment is small and the audit closes soon after the policy expires.

A Monthly Checklist

  • Payroll reported only for jobs with an approved classification.
  • Every job reporting payroll mapped to a host employer, a jobsite and a state.
  • Overtime premium shown separately in payroll records.
  • New states confirmed with the agent before work starts there.
  • At quarter-end, reported payroll reconciled to Form 941 wages, by FEIN.
  • At year-end, Forms 1099-NEC reviewed for workers who may be employees.

None of this requires new software. It requires deciding that the audit is something the company prepares for every month.
