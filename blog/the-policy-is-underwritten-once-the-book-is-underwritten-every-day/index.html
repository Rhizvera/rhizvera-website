The Policy Is Underwritten Once. The Book Is Underwritten Every Day.

Why classification drifts in staffing programs, and what a carrier or program can require to keep daily placements in line with underwriting intent

Rhizvera · November 28, 2026

Who this is for: carriers, programs, general agents, captives, PEOs and staffing EORs that insure or support temporary staffing companies.

A workers’ compensation program that insures temporary staffing companies makes a careful decision at the start of each policy. The underwriter reviews the submission, the loss history, the experience modification factor (mod), the classifications and the payroll. The account is accepted, priced and bound.

Then the staffing company goes to work. In the first month it may sign new customers, open jobs at new jobsites and move workers into duties nobody described in the submission. Each of those is an underwriting decision. Most of them are made at a branch, by someone whose job is to fill the order.

That is where classification drift begins.

What Drift Looks Like

Drift is rarely one large error. It is a series of small, reasonable-looking choices:

  • A new customer’s job is coded the way a similar customer’s job was coded last year.
  • A worker approved for packing is asked to cover a forklift shift “just for today.”
  • A jobsite moves to a new building with different equipment, and nobody reports the change.
  • The customer supplies a code from its own policy, and the branch uses it without checking the duties.
  • Payroll is reported under the class code the branch expected, not the one the job supports.

None of these looks dramatic. Together they move the book away from what the underwriter priced. The program finds out at a claim, at the final audit, or when loss ratios stop matching the rates.

Why the Submission Can’t Carry the Year

In NCCI states, temporary workers placed with a client are generally classified the same as the client’s own employees doing the same or similar work (NCCI Basic Manual Rule 1-D-3-f), with state exceptions. The classification of a staffing account is therefore not settled when the policy is bound. It is set job by job, as customers are added.

A manufacturer’s operations may change a few times a year. A staffing company’s exposure can change with every job order. A submission describes the book on the day it was written. It cannot underwrite customers the staffing company has not met yet.

Where the Underwriting Actually Happens

For a staffing program, the most important underwriting unit is not the staffing company. It is the combination of three things, and the class code follows from them:

  • The host employer: who the customer is, what it does, and how it manages safety.
  • The jobsite: where the work happens, which may not belong to the host employer, and which state’s rules apply.
  • The job: the actual duties, equipment, materials, supervision and training.

A program that sees only payroll totals by class code is looking at the result of those decisions, not the decisions themselves.

What a Program Can Require

Programs differ in authority, appetite and capacity. The controls below are common practice in well-run staffing programs and can be adapted to any of them.

  • A determination before placement. No worker starts until the host employer, jobsite and job have been reviewed and a class code approved by someone with authority to approve it.
  • A documented basis. Each determination records the duties reviewed, who reviewed them and why the code fits, so the decision can be checked later.
  • Coverage confirmed for the state. No approval for a jobsite in a state where the staffing company has no active workers’ compensation coverage on the start date.
  • Change control. A change in duties, equipment, location or shift triggers a new review, not a phone call.
  • Feedback from audits and claims. When an audit or a claim shows a job was coded wrong, the correction is checked against other open jobs on the same code, not just the one file.

What It Asks of the Staffing Company

None of this is meant to slow placements. A staffing company has to fill orders quickly, and a review that takes days will be worked around. The goal is a process fast enough that following it is easier than skipping it. Most determinations are routine. The ones that are not routine are exactly the ones a program wants to see before a worker is on the floor.

For the staffing company, a documented determination is also protection. When an auditor or adjuster questions a code months later, the record shows what was described, what was approved and by whom.

Questions Worth Asking

  • For any worker on assignment today, can you show which approved job they are working under?
  • Who approved each open job, and was it someone outside the branch that filled it?
  • When an audit reclassifies a job, what happens to the other open jobs on the same code?
  • How do you learn that a customer’s operations have changed?

If those answers take a week to assemble, the book is being underwritten by whoever fills the order.
