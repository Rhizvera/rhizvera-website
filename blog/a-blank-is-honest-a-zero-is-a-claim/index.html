A Blank Is Honest. A Zero Is a Claim.

How missing, estimated and unverified figures distort staffing program reports, and the reporting rules that keep them honest

Rhizvera · November 28, 2026

Who this is for: carriers, programs, captives, general agents and staffing company owners who read program reports and dashboards.

A report arrives showing a staffing company’s payroll by month. One month shows zero. Does that mean the company had no payroll, or that nobody reported it? The report cannot tell you. Whoever reads it will assume one or the other, and the decisions that follow will be built on that assumption.

In workers’ compensation programs for temporary staffing, decisions about pricing, renewal, collateral and audits rest on figures like these. When a report cannot tell the difference between “none” and “unknown,” it is not reporting. It is guessing.

Three Ways Figures Mislead

  • A zero where data is missing. A blank field turned into a zero becomes a statement of fact: no payroll, no claims, no exposure.
  • An estimate dressed as a measurement. A projected payroll or a modeled loss figure shown in the same column as reported figures, with no label, gets treated as actual.
  • A total over part of the data. A program total that leaves out a company whose report has not arrived looks complete and is not. Loss ratios, averages and rankings built on it are wrong, and usually in the same direction.

Reported Is Not Verified

Staffing payroll moves through stages. It is reported by the staffing company, reconciled against tax filings, reviewed at audit and finally settled. A report that mixes those stages without saying which is which overstates how much is known. Readers should be able to see whether a figure is reported, reconciled or audited.

Rules Worth Adopting

  • Show missing data as missing. Use a dash or “not reported,” never zero.
  • Label estimates. If a figure is projected or modeled, say so beside it, and keep it out of totals of actual figures.
  • Total only what is complete. If a total cannot include every item it claims to cover, mark it incomplete or leave it out.
  • Say what the reader is seeing. A summary built from part of a book should never be presented as the whole book.
  • Keep the source. Every figure should trace back to the report, filing or document it came from.
  • Don’t fill the gap with a guess. A number that cannot be verified from a legitimate source is better recorded as absent than invented.

The Cost of Looking Complete

Reports that always look complete are comfortable. Every cell has a number and every total adds up. But a program that renews an account on a loss ratio that left out two late reports, or sets collateral on an estimated payroll nobody labeled, has taken on risk it did not see.

A report with honest blanks is less comfortable and more useful. It shows the reader exactly where the work is unfinished.

For Staffing Company Owners

The same rules apply inside a staffing company. A branch dashboard that shows zero injuries because injuries were not entered is worse than no dashboard. When you present figures to an insurer, a lender or a buyer, label what is reported, what is verified and what is estimated. That builds a credibility a polished but unexplained number cannot.
