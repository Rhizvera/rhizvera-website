Everyone Needs Part of the File

How staffing companies and their programs can share information with agents, insurers, funders and auditors without losing control of it

Rhizvera · November 28, 2026

Who this is for: staffing company owners, and the carriers, programs, agents and service providers who work with them.

A temporary staffing company’s records are read by more outside parties than most businesses’. The agent needs them to market the account. The carrier or program needs them to underwrite and audit. A general agent, captive manager, TPA, payroll funder, lender, outside auditor and, one day, a buyer may each need a part.

In most staffing companies, these parties get what they need the same way: by email, as spreadsheets and PDFs, sent by whoever was asked.

What Goes Wrong

  • More is sent than needed. A lender asking whether coverage is in force receives the full payroll file. An agent marketing one state receives every customer’s name.
  • Nobody knows what went where. Six months later, no one can say which version was sent, to whom, or why.
  • Access never ends. An agent replaced last year still holds this year’s customer list in its inbox.
  • The wrong person decides. A branch manager or recruiter releases records that only an owner or officer should release.
  • Customer information leaks. Host employer names, bill rates and jobsite details are a staffing company’s competitive core.

Principles for Sharing

  • The staffing company owns its information. Sharing it with an agent, insurer or service provider to underwrite or run the business is legitimate, and the company decides what is shared.
  • Share what the role needs. A payroll funder may need to know coverage is in force without seeing claim details. A prospective buyer may need summaries long before it needs customer names.
  • Put authority with an owner or officer. Decide in advance who may approve releasing records to an outside party, and write it down.
  • Keep a record of what was shared: the date, the recipient, what was sent and why.
  • End access when the relationship ends. When an agent, funder or provider changes, stop sending updates, and ask for return or deletion where the agreement allows.
  • Draw the line by date. An outgoing agent reasonably keeps the history from the years it represented you; what comes after belongs to the new agent.

For Carriers and Programs

Programs sit at the center of this exchange and hold more of a staffing company’s information than anyone else. Program staff should see what their role requires. Reports to outside stakeholders, such as a fronting carrier or a captive’s board, should carry what each needs and no more. Firm walls between staffing companies, and between programs, protect everyone’s confidential information.

When the Company Is Sold

Information discipline matters most in a sale. Buyers ask for everything. A company that shares in stages, holding back customer names until terms are agreed and keeping a log of every document provided, stays in control of the process and protects its customer relationships if the deal does not close. When the buyer is a competitor, the Federal Trade Commission warns that sharing competitively sensitive information before closing can raise antitrust problems; a clean team that reviews sensitive detail apart from the buyer’s business people is a common safeguard.

Start With an Inventory

List the outside parties that receive your records today, what each receives, who sends it and who approved it. Most staffing companies find at least one party receiving more than it needs, and at least one that should no longer receive anything.
