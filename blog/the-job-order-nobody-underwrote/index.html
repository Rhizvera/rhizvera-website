The Job Order Nobody Underwrote

How the handoff between sales, account management and recruiting creates workers’ compensation risk, and how to close the gap

Rhizvera · November 28, 2026

Who this is for: staffing company owners and operations leaders, and the programs that insure them.

A temporary staffing company runs on three functions. Sales finds the customer. Account management keeps the customer and takes the orders. Recruiting finds the people. Each function is measured on its own results, and each can do its job well.

The trouble starts in the spaces between them.

The Handoff Problem

A salesperson wins a new account after a meeting with the customer’s operations manager. The job is described as light assembly. The account manager writes up the order. The recruiter reads the order and starts calling candidates.

By the time the first worker arrives, the description has passed through three people, and each version is a little shorter than the last. “Light assembly at a bench” at the meeting becomes “assembly” on the order and “production” in the recruiter’s notes. Nobody has misled anyone. The information has simply worn down in transit.

Workers’ compensation classification depends on the actual duties. When the duties are described loosely, the class code is chosen loosely.

Job Changes Nobody Approved

The second problem comes after the worker starts. The customer’s supervisor needs someone on a machine, on a ladder or on a forklift. The temporary worker is there and willing. The change is made on the floor, often without anyone at the staffing company knowing.

The worker is now doing a job that was never reviewed, that may carry a different class code, and that may require training the worker never received. If an injury follows, the question will be what the staffing company knew and what it approved.

Recruiters Know What Is Really Happening

Recruiters and on-site coordinators often hear first that a job has changed. Workers tell them about the new duties, the missing equipment, the supervisor who pushes too hard. In many firms that information stays with the recruiter, because nobody asks for it and there is no place to put it. OSHA and NIOSH recommend that staffing agencies give workers a written way to report when a host employer changes their tasks.

That is valuable intelligence about the staffing company’s own risk, and it is usually lost.

The Fill-Rate Trap

When sales is paid on new accounts and recruiting is paid on fill rate, every incentive points toward placing someone quickly. Pausing an order to confirm duties or classification feels like a cost to everyone involved. A company that rewards only speed should not be surprised when classification becomes an afterthought.

Make the Job the Center of the Process

The fix is not another layer of approval. It is a shared record, built around the job, that all three functions work from.

  • One job record. Duties, equipment, location, shift, supervision and training are written once, in the same fields, and everyone uses that record.
  • An approval gate. No worker is placed until the job has been reviewed and its classification approved.
  • Change control. A change in duties, location, equipment or shift goes back through review before the worker performs it. Customers are told this in the contract and at the start of every account.
  • A closed feedback loop. What recruiters and workers report about a job is recorded against that job and reviewed.
  • Shared measures. Sales, account management and recruiting are measured together on each account’s results, including claims and audit findings, not only on wins and fills.
  • Claims tied to the job. When an injury happens, it is connected back to the original job record, so the company can see whether the job changed before the injury.

Decide Who May Change a Job

Every staffing company should be able to answer one question without hesitation: who may approve a change in a temporary worker’s duties? If the honest answer is “the customer’s supervisor,” the staffing company has handed its underwriting to someone who does not carry its insurance.

Why Programs Care

Carriers and programs that insure staffing companies see the result of this disconnect in their claims and audits. A staffing company that can show one job record, a review before placement and a working change-control process is easier to underwrite, and easier to defend when a claim or a code is questioned.
